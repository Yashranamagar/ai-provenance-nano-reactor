# Critical Reflection — "Nano Reactor" Campaign

*Individual track · AIGC 5604 Capstone. Addresses copyright, bias, deepfakes/synthetic media, environmental impact, and human creative control. ~1,200 words.*

## Overview
The *Nano Reactor* campaign advertises a fictional compact nuclear module — the NR-114 — as clean, on-site power for the AI data centers driving today's compute boom. It was produced end-to-end with generative AI under close human direction: **Claude Design** for the poster template and brand system; **Google Flow** (Nano Banana 2) for all poster imagery and the hero reactor; **Google Flow** (Veo 3.1 Lite/Fast and Omni Flash) for the video, using an image-to-video pipeline; **ElevenLabs** (Eleven Multilingual v2) for the voiceover; and **Suno** (v4.5-all) for the score, all assembled by hand in **DaVinci Resolve**. Working this way surfaced real ethical questions, which I address below.

## 1. Copyright, Ownership, and the Line Between Inspiration and Copying
Two named sources shaped the work: **Aalo Atomics** (aalo.com) for the compact-reactor product concept, and a **DJI product poster** for the background layout and cinematic mood. This raises the central question of where inspiration ends and derivative infringement begins.

I drew that line deliberately. Neither source image was ever copied, traced, or composited into the deliverables. The DJI poster informed *composition principles* — hero object in the lower third, generous negative space for typography, dark-to-warm gradient — not pixels; the final background was generated from scratch by Nano Banana 2. The Aalo influence is conceptual: a cylindrical, "walk-away safe" small modular reactor framed as trustworthy industrial hardware, not a reproduction of Aalo's branding or product renders. I disclosed both sources openly in `ethical_licensing_audit.csv` rather than hiding them, because transparent provenance is what separates homage from passing-off.

Ownership of the AI outputs is more genuinely uncertain, and here I found a concrete limit worth naming: the music was generated on **Suno's free plan, which does not grant commercial rights**. Because this portfolio is a non-commercial academic exercise, that use is acceptable in context — but I have documented it openly in the licensing audit, and for any real release the track would need a paid Suno plan or a commercially-licensed replacement. The remaining tools (Claude Design, Google Flow, ElevenLabs) I have flagged as "to verify" rather than assumed. The brand mark is fully synthetic and speculative; because "Nano Reactor" is fictional, I also checked that the generated logo does not closely imitate a real energy company's mark. Being specific about *where the licensing actually fails* is, I think, more honest than a blanket "all rights cleared" claim.

## 2. Bias
Text-to-image and text-to-video models carry visual biases baked into their training data — a default aesthetic of what "advanced," "premium," or "industrial" technology looks like. Across dozens of generations, my outputs consistently returned a particular look: cold blue-and-charcoal palettes, sleek dark metal, Western hyperscale-datacenter architecture, and a glossy commercial sheen. Peer reviewers noticed this: two independently compared the result to "an Apple hardware ad" and flagged the aesthetic as "familiar," and originality was among my lower-scoring criteria (avg 4.0). That familiarity *is* the bias — the model gravitates to a dominant, homogenized vision of high-tech futurism. I mitigated it through human curation (selecting, rejecting, re-directing) and image-conditioned edits that locked my own hero design, but I did not fully escape the model's default idiom, and I would push harder against it next time.

## 3. Deepfakes and Synthetic Media
The voiceover is entirely synthetic, but importantly it is a **stock synthetic voice ("Kal Jones"), not a clone of a real, identifiable person**. That distinction matters: no real individual's likeness, consent, or identity is implicated, so the deepfake harms most associated with voice cloning — impersonation, misattribution, fraud — do not apply here. The video imagery is likewise of a fictional product and unpeopled industrial scenes.

That said, the whole spot is synthetic media presenting a product that does not exist, in a photoreal commercial register designed to feel credible. I believe advertising built from generative AI should be disclosed as such to viewers, and this portfolio does so throughout. The interesting production detail is that making the synthetic voice usable required a **pronunciation script**: I rewrote copy so the model would enunciate correctly — "NR-114" became "N. R. one one four," with added periods and ellipses to control pacing. This is a small but honest illustration of how much human authorship sits inside "AI-generated" audio.

## 4. Environmental Impact
There is an unavoidable irony at the center of this project: it is a campaign *about* clean energy for AI, produced *with* energy-intensive generative AI. I generated well over fifty images and video clips across multiple models — each Veo render and Nano Banana iteration carries real compute cost, and video generation is far heavier than text or still images. It would be dishonest to advertise "zero operational carbon" while ignoring the footprint of the advertisement itself.

I take two things from this. First, the campaign's own premise — that AI's power demand is outpacing the infrastructure built to supply it — is not just marketing copy; it described my own workflow in miniature. Second, iteration discipline is an environmental practice, not only a creative one: re-rolling generations carelessly has a cost, so working from a locked base image and making targeted conversational edits (rather than mass-generating variants) is the more responsible method as well as the more controllable one.

## 5. Human Creative Control
The strongest evidence that this is authored work, not push-button output, is in the process logs. I made every art-direction decision: the concept and its Aalo/DJI references; the hero reactor's cylindrical form; the selection among iterations; the spec messaging and typography; the resize to submission spec; and the peer-driven corrections. The prompts themselves are authored artifacts — my video prompts deliberately art-direct the reactor's cooling and core as **"calm, controlled thermal management rather than danger,"** with explicit negative prompts against "sparks," "warning signals," and "damage." Finally, the entire spot was cut, sequenced, and synced by hand in DaVinci Resolve; the AI produced raw shots, but the edit — pacing, order, and the marriage of image, voice, and music — is mine.

## 6. Responding to the Ethics Critique (Peer Feedback)
Ethics was my lowest-scored dimension (avg 3.60; one reviewer gave 2/5). The consistent concern was **greenwashing**: strong, unqualified claims — "walk-away safe," "zero op. carbon," "clean" nuclear — presented as fact in marketing copy. I think this critique is correct and valuable. In a real campaign these claims would demand evidence and qualification; presenting them as absolutes is exactly the rhetorical move that makes green marketing untrustworthy. My response here is honesty about register: this is a **speculative, fictional** product, labeled as such throughout the documentation, and the critique itself is preserved in `peer_review_revision_log.csv` rather than smoothed over. If I extended the work, I would temper the on-artifact claims or add a visible "speculative concept" qualifier.

## 7. What I Would Do Differently
- **Push past the model's default aesthetic** to answer the "looks familiar" critique with a more distinctive visual language.
- **Temper or qualify the sustainability claims** on the artifact itself, not only in the writeup.
- **Track compute cost** as a first-class metric alongside creative iterations.
- **Lock licensing up front** — confirm commercial/ownership terms for each tool before building, not after.
